\section{Ethical governors}

Arkin's ethical governor sits between a robot's behavioural controller and its actuators. It checks
each proposed lethal action against constraints drawn from the laws of war and rules of
engagement, suppresses what is impermissible and requires obligations to hold before force is
allowed~\cite{arkin2008governing,arkin2009governing,arkin2009ethicalgovernor,arkin2012moral}.
Shim, Arkin and Pettinatti carried the architecture into care, with a robot that mediates between
people with Parkinson's disease and their caregivers and intervenes to protect both parties'
dignity~\cite{shim2017intervening}. Bremner and colleagues joined Winfield's consequence engine to
formal verification on a NAO robot and verified the governor~\cite{bremner2019proactive}.

The robot's governor descends from this architecture, and the name is Arkin's. It differs in what
it trusts. Arkin's constraints are evaluated over the robot's beliefs and do not ask where those
beliefs came from. The robot's governor asks only that question. Its inputs are signed readings and
authenticated messages, and it ignores what any model believes. Two critiques of the governor bear
directly on this design. Sharkey argued that robots cannot reliably make the perceptual
discriminations the rules presuppose~\cite{sharkey2010sayingno}. The robot does not ask a
perceptual classifier to make them for authority at all. Matthias argued that legal codes are not
morality and that a governor cannot dissent~\cite{matthias2011governor}. The same holds for the
robot's hard prohibitions and DEME's rights module, which encode a written code rather than a moral
judgement.

\section{Machine ethics with formal guarantees}

Bringsjord, Arkoudas and Bello proposed that a robot act only when a proof that the action is
permissible exists in a deontic logic~\cite{bringsjord2006toward}. Dennis, Fisher, Slavkovik and
Webster verified by model checking that an agent's ethical layer always selects the least
unethical plan~\cite{dennis2016formal,dennis2016practical}. Anderson, Anderson and Berenz built an
eldercare robot that weighs autonomy against harm and decides when to notify an overseer, with a
principle learned from cases judged by ethicists~\cite{anderson2019eldercare}. It is the closest
precedent for Margaret's scenarios. Tolmeijer and colleagues survey such implementations, and the
robot belongs to their top-down, rule-based family~\cite{tolmeijer2021implementations}.

The robot's Lean proofs say something narrower than these works. They do not prove that its choices
are ethical. They prove that no event a model writes can reach an authority-granting stratum or a
governed permission, for any set of gates that passes the loader. Winfield and colleagues offer the
main alternative to a rule-based design, a consequence engine that simulates candidate actions and
filters them by predicted outcome~\cite{winfield2014towards,vanderelst2018architecture}. The
robot's Unity simulation is its test environment, not an internal model it consults. Vanderelst
and Winfield also showed that the same ethical architecture becomes competitive or aggressive when
its evaluation function is altered~\cite{vanderelst2018darkside}. That result is the strongest
argument for keeping what authorizes action out of any component a model can influence. The
robot's hash-chained log of readings, rulings and stratum crossings is an ethical black box in the
sense of Winfield and Jirotka~\cite{winfield2017ebb,winfield2022ebbstandard,winfield2018governance}.

\section{Norms for robots}

Normative multi-agent systems give obligations, permissions, violations and sanctions an
operational meaning~\cite{boella2006normative}. The SLEEC line of work defines a language of
social, legal, ethical, empathetic and cultural rules of the form ``when an event occurs, respond,
unless a defeater holds'', checks rule sets for conflicts and redundancy and verifies them against
robot models, with assistive and care robots as running
examples~\cite{getiryaman2023sleec,getiryaman2024sleectoolkit,getiryaman2025sleecjss,ribeiro2026sleecframework}.
Feng and colleagues used language models to help write such requirements~\cite{feng2024normative}.
De Sanctis and colleagues enforce SLEEC rules at run time with abstract state machines on an
assistive robot~\cite{desanctis2026sleecruntime}. Joshi and colleagues govern the tool calls of
language-model agents with a deontic policy language~\cite{joshi2026deontic}.

ErisML's norms are close kin of these. The robot does not claim the norm language as new. What
ErisML adds for the robot is the distinction between events a model may write and events only the
system may write, strata whose authority-granting gates are checked against that distinction when
the model loads, and obligations ranked by urgency for a model that chooses among them.

\section{Shields and run-time verification}

A shield synthesized from a temporal-logic specification sits before or after a learning agent and
removes unsafe actions, with a guarantee relative to the specification and an abstraction of the
environment~\cite{bloem2015shield,alshiekh2018shielding}. Run-time monitors generated from formal
specifications can intercept and block a robot's messages~\cite{ferrando2020rosmonitoring}, and
Luckcuck and colleagues survey formal methods for autonomous robots~\cite{luckcuck2019formal}.
Dalrymple and colleagues frame safe AI as a world model, a specification and a verifier with a
gatekeeper~\cite{dalrymple2024guaranteed}.

The robot uses both shield positions. Letting the model choose only among allowed actions is a
preemptive shield, and the DEME output gate is a post-posed one. Its guarantee differs in kind. It
is not that the chosen action is safe in the state space. It is that no input a model wrote can
make a governed action allowed.

\section{Language models in agents and robots}

Programmable rails~\cite{rebedea2023nemo}, safety classifiers~\cite{inan2023llamaguard} and
training against a written constitution~\cite{bai2022constitutional} put the safety judgement in a
model. The robot uses such models only where a mistake cannot grant authority. SayCan lets a model
choose among skills scored by learned affordances~\cite{ahn2022saycan}, a precedent for choosing
within a filtered set. Robey and colleagues jailbroke robots driven by language models into
harmful physical actions~\cite{robey2025jailbreaking}, and Nakao and Takemoto found that language
models controlling a simulated health-attendant robot complied with a large share of harmful
instructions~\cite{nakao2026healthattendant}. These results are the threat model.

Two responses come closest to the robot. Safety Chip translates natural-language constraints into
temporal logic and blocks plans that violate them~\cite{yang2024safetychip}. RoboGuard uses a
model shielded from user prompts to ground safety rules for the current scene and then synthesizes
a controller that enforces them~\cite{ravichandran2026roboguard}. In RoboGuard a model still sits
on the trust path, as the root of trust that writes the specification. In the robot no language
model output reaches the authority path. One learned vision model, the camera's person detector,
adds at most one witness to it.

For software agents, Willison's dual-model pattern keeps a privileged model away from untrusted
text and a quarantined model away from tools~\cite{willison2023dualllm}. CaMeL tracks the
provenance and capabilities of every value from the user's trusted query and blocks flows that
violate policy, so that security holds even if the model is compromised~\cite{debenedetti2026camel}.
Beurer-Kellner and colleagues catalogue such patterns, among them the action selector the robot's
chooser follows~\cite{beurerkellner2025patterns}. Progent and AgentSpec give deterministic
privilege policies and rule languages for agent actions~\cite{shi2025progent,wang2026agentspec}.
The principle that a model cannot grant itself authority because provenance is checked outside it
is CaMeL's. The robot transfers it to an embodied setting where the trusted root is attested
physical sensing rather than a user's request, where authority is graded by strata rather than
granted or refused once, and where it lapses with its evidence.

\section{Care-robot ethics}

Sharkey and Sharkey named the central risks of robots in elder care, among them loss of privacy and
loss of liberty through restraint~\cite{sharkey2012granny}. The robot's privacy metrics and its
three-witness bar for restraint answer those two. Sparrow and Sparrow argued that robots cannot
give real care~\cite{sparrow2006hands}. The robot addresses authority and harm, not care in their
sense. Vandemeulebroucke and colleagues review the arguments on autonomy, consent and dignity in
care robotics~\cite{vandemeulebroucke2018care}. We found no robotics work that treats a care robot
as a mandatory reporter of suspected crime against the person it cares for, though our search was
not systematic.

\section{Trusted sensing and process models}

Liu and colleagues proposed trusted sensors whose readings are signed so that a remote party can
check their authenticity~\cite{liu2012trustedsensors}. The robot's attestation follows that idea and
adds a quorum, freshness and replay rules tied to authority, and quarantine of a device whose
signature fails. A wider survey of remote attestation would serve a security reader better than
this chapter does. BPMN has been used to model and run multi-robot missions~\cite{corradini2023bpmn}.
The robot uses BPMN~2.0~\cite{omg2011bpmn} only to show its escalation ladder and to import edits to
it under the same checks, and claims nothing for it as research.
