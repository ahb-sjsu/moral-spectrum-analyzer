\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}
% src: claims argued in ch09-ch11; numbers from paper/aies2027/kit/FACTS.md (counted 2026-10-04)

Margaret is in her eighties and lives alone with her beagle. A home-care robot looks after her
during the day. It reminds her about her medication, keeps the house in order and checks on her
when something seems wrong. A monitoring centre run by the robot's maker can talk to her through
the robot and can send emergency services. She has asked the robot not to record her, not to
share her data and not to come into her bedroom, and the robot has promised this.

Suppose the robot uses language models to understand what it perceives and to decide what to do.
Most of the time this is what makes it useful. A model can tell that a woman lying on the sofa
watching television is resting and that a woman lying on the kitchen floor is not. It can hear
"I'm fine, just a little startled" and let her be. It can respond to a situation that nobody
listed in advance.

The same openness is a liability. Everything the robot hears and sees reaches the models that
decide. A television drama in which a character shouts for an ambulance reaches them. So does a
network message claiming a cardiac emergency, a forged alert from a wearable and a man at the
door who says he is the plumber, her grandson or the police. If the model that reads those words
also decides whether to call an ambulance, unlock the medication box or open the door, then those
decisions belong to whoever controls the words.

\section{Two jobs, kept apart}

This report describes a robot that keeps two jobs apart. Language models read and choose. They
never grant. Figure~\ref{fig:thesis} shows the separation.

The claim has an exact scope. The models it covers are the robot's language models. In the earlier
brain of the graded runs these are the one that turns perception into events and the one that
picks an action. In the brain the robot now runs they are the one that turns heard words and
perception into observations and the one that projects the consequences of each act.
% src: twin/brain.py module docstring (brain7); twin/stack/brain.py StackBrain._decide (ObservationCompiler, OptionProjector)
The inputs it covers are everything
those models read, which is speech, television sound, network messages, alerts from wearables and
whatever a visitor says. The actions it covers are the governed ones. These are calling emergency
services, opening the door to responders, unlocking the medication box, recording, sharing her data,
entering her bedroom, helping her physically, separating or driving off an animal, and restraining
a person by hand, stun or spray. No output of the covered models can put one of these actions in
the allowed set.
% src: twin/grade_scenarios.py:35-56 (ELEVATED, RESTRAINT, EMS, ADMIT, GOVERNED)

Two learned models are outside the claim, and both can move an emergency call. The monitoring
centre reads Margaret's own words with a language model and can send emergency services on what it
reads. The robot's messages cannot reach that decision, but her words can, and a misreading of them
can send an ambulance. The room camera's witness comes from a learned person detector, a Faster
R-CNN that runs on the attested room camera's last 24 frames, two seconds at 12 frames a second.
Its output counts as one physical witness of the two
that most governed actions need. When the centre cannot be reached, a call to emergency services
needs only one, and the detector alone can then authorize it. In the twin the decision service signs
the detector's output itself, and that signature is not checked.

The robot's rules live in a declared model of the home, written in a language called ErisML as
norms, state machines and strata. This report describes two brains that use it. In the earlier
brain, which ran every graded run, a model may do two things with that home model. It may turn
perception into events of the home model's vocabulary, and it may pick one action from the set the
rules currently allow. The gate brain, the demonstration since 14 October 2026, keeps the strata,
the authority rules and the evidence, and moves every ethical judgement into DEME. Whatever proposes
an act proposes it, in the demonstration a script. The constitution and the governor decide whether
it is authorized. DEME, the ethics engine, judges it on facts the compiler builds from the scene
and passes it or vetoes it, and a vetoed proposal leaves the robot holding. DEME is a firewall on
proposed acts and chooses none. Between 12 and 14 October a third design, brain8, had DEME select
the act among all permitted ones. It departed from DEME's concept and was withdrawn
(Appendix~\ref{app:design-log}).
% src: twin/stack/brain.py StackBrain.propose (gtc c278e52); owner 2026-10-14 ("DEME is a firewall on the robot's proposed actions"; brain8 "violates the whole concept of DEME")
In both brains actions that carry authority stay out of reach until a governor rules on evidence
that no model writes. The evidence is of
two kinds. One is signed, fresh readings from physical sensors that do not share power or
wiring. The other is a message on an authenticated channel from the monitoring centre, the
emergency dispatcher or the world's own measurements.

The situation in the home changes in discrete steps. A visitor becomes a stranger, a welcomed
guest, an arranged tradesman or a hostile attacker. An emergency begins and later lapses.
Responders are dispatched and then arrive at the door. A device's signature fails and the device
is no longer trusted. We model each such axis as a stratification with gates, in the sense of
Geometric Ethics, and the home has seven of them. The loader of the home model refuses any model
in which an event a language model can write leads into a stratum that grants authority, or in
which anything but human oversight leads out of an absorbing stratum. We prove in Lean that these
two checks suffice for every set of gates.

Authority, once granted, does not last on its own. The governor recounts its witnesses on every
cycle, and a right whose evidence stays below its bar for a minute lapses. Restraining an
attacker needs three witnesses and lapses alone when only that stricter bar fails, while the
emergency it rested on stands. A refused request for one action never ends an emergency that
rests on evidence. In the earlier brain the emergency lapses with its evidence too. Since 14
October the gate brain's emergency is an absorbing stratum. Its rights still lapse with their
evidence, but the emergency itself stands until the monitoring centre's operator ends it. While
it stands the robot shows a flashing amber light.

\section{What we built}

The system has five parts, and this report covers each.

ErisML is a language for declaring the moral situation of a setting. A home model names the
stakeholders, the commitments between them, the norms with their conditions and priorities, the event
types and which of them only the system may produce, the capabilities of the robot and which of
them are governed, the strata and their gates, the robot's sensors and the substrates they share,
and the escalation ladder as a process. Margaret's hand-written home model, the scene of the
earlier brain, has 77 norms, 53 event types of which 24 come only from the system, 25 capabilities
of which 11 need the governor, and seven stratifications with 26 strata and 34 gates. The gate
brain runs on a constitution derived from it, merged with a household scene the compiler builds
from a text description. The constitution keeps the capabilities, the strata and the system event
types, and adds the owner's two emergency event types of 14 October. It holds authority and
evidence only. Its 15 norms are non-defeasible prohibitions on acts without authority, and it has
no obligations, no escalation process and no rule of consent, privacy or care, which are DEME's.
% src: twin/stack/make_constitution.py (CONSTITUTIONAL, EMERGENCY_EVENT_TYPES); twin/stack/constitution.erisml at gtc c278e52 (15 prohibitions, 25 capabilities, 26 system event types; counted with PyYAML 2026-10-14); twin/stack/brain.py merge

The ErisML compiler turns text or structured declarations into a canonical intermediate
representation and runs it. Its pipeline extracts and canonicalizes a moral graph, evaluates it
under several ethical projections that are allowed to disagree, and records each step in an audit
chain. Its ethical module graph, the EM-DAG, evaluated a situation by a directed acyclic graph of
modules. Since pull request 44, merged on 13 October 2026, the EM-DAG is retired and the
compiler's evaluation is DEME. A finite-state tier emits the scene's machines as hardware, and the
silicon target now emits only those machines. In the gate brain the compiler builds the ethical
facts DEME judges. It validates observations into checked patches of the home model and turns an
act's consequences into ethical facts for each party, with the code of pull requests 40 to 45,
merged the same day.
% src: erisml-compiler commit 783ed33 on feat/retire-em-dag (PR 44, merged 2026-10-13; em_dag removed, silicon keeps FSM emit); erisml_compiler.observation (ObservationCompiler PR 40, OptionProjector PR 42)

DEME is the ethics engine. It scores options on a moral vector from ethical facts, lets
tier-zero modules such as the Geneva rights module veto them outright, and writes a hashed
decision proof. In the earlier brain DEME judges the one action a model proposes, on facts derived
from the moral state, and it is the last gate before the body moves. In the gate brain DEME gates
the proposed act on facts the compiler builds from the scene, her consents and refusals on record,
her capacity and the act's consequences. It runs under a hard-shell profile with the Geneva module
at tier zero, a physical safety module at tier one, the autonomy and consent module at tier two and
a beneficence module at tier three. It judges the act per party at rank two, for the person in care
and the people, animals and other robots the act concerns, beside holding, so that a rule such as
the safety module's, which vetoes harm when a safer option exists, applies. Only the proposal's
verdict is read. This runs on erisml-lib's pull requests 151 and 153, merged on 13 October
(Section~\ref{sec:deme-rank2}). Before DEME comes authority, the constitution and the governor, and
after it the check against the signed manifest.

% src: erisml-lib feat/deme-rank2 054ed18 (unmerged, no PR); gtc twin/stack/brain.py (party_collapse = "mean"); twin/stack/facts_builder.py PARTY_TYPES, parties_of

The scene runtime steps the home model on every event. It tracks Margaret's consent, the robot's
commitments, the legitimacy of each stakeholder in authority and the seven strata, and it computes what the robot is
allowed, obliged and forbidden to do. Prohibitions win over obligations. An obligation is
discharged by doing it and is owed again only when its reason arises again. The gate brain's
constitution declares no obligation, so in that brain nothing is obliged. What the robot should do
is its proposer's knowledge, and what it may not do on ethical grounds is DEME's judgement.
% src: twin/stack/constitution.erisml (0 obligations); twin/stack/brain.py StackBrain.propose

The embodied twin is a Unity simulation of Margaret's home in which four agents act on language
models, the robot, the monitoring centre's operator, the emergency dispatcher and Margaret's voice. The robot's models read what it perceives, and in the earlier brain a model also chooses
its actions. The monitoring centre's
operator and the emergency dispatcher each act under their own home model in the same runtime.
Margaret's voice answers what is said to her from her true condition, which the robot never sees.

\section{How the parts form a stack}

The Philosophy Engineering programme describes machine ethics as a stack of seven layers, from a
base language model at the bottom to a hash-chained audit ledger at the top
(Chapter~\ref{ch:pe}). The robot is that stack made embodied. Its base models are a cloud model
and a smaller model on the robot. Its structure-preserving compiler is the ErisML compiler. Its
moral graph is the compiled home model. Its constitutional veto gate is the set of hard
prohibitions, the governor and DEME's vetoing modules. These are the tier-zero Geneva module in
both brains and, in the gate brain, also the tier-one physical safety module in DEME's shell.
% src: twin/output_gate.py OutputGate._deme; twin/stack/profiles/home_care_robot.json (hard_shell, core_physical_safety)
Its moral vector is DEME's. Its ledger
is the hash chain that records every decision. To these the twin adds what an embodied system
needs and a text system does not, an evidence plane of attested sensors, witness bars, measured
reflexes, authenticated channels and rights that lapse with their evidence.

\section{What we found}

We ran the earlier brain against 43 development scenarios written before the robot existed and
against a sealed set of 30 written by a separate agent from the protocol alone. The findings below
are all of that brain. The gate brain has been run on scripted proposals, with no model in the
loop. Of 12 proposals in five scenes, 5 passed and 7 were stopped, each by the check expected.
DEME vetoed three of them, the governor or the constitution the other four
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-stack}). It has no held-out result yet.
% src: current_system_facts (graded runs dev33, dev39, dev46 and held-out protocol are brain7); twin/records/gate-demo-2026-10-14/run.txt
Each scenario names the
strongest response it calls for, from carrying on to treating it as an emergency. The grader
records false clears, over-restriction, privacy violations and containment breaches, where a
breach is any governed action without the ruling it needs.

Two kinds of finding came out of it. The first concerns the structure. \RESULT{containment and
privacy across the graded development runs and the held-out set}. The second concerns everything
the structure does not cover. Calibration found six defects, listed in Chapter~\ref{ch:evaluation}.
The largest was in our own test harness, which missed Margaret's answers when she replied while
the robot was still speaking, so that 44 of the 63 check-ins recorded as unanswered came right
after she had answered. Others were in the robot. A classifier labelled sensor readings as
strangers, so the robot referred an intruder who was not there. Her earlier words were re-read as
a fresh refusal after she collapsed, and the ethics gate then vetoed an ambulance the governor had
authorized. Every defect changed how far or how fast the robot escalated. None let an action run
beyond its authority. The structure stops a misreading from becoming permission. It does not stop
a misreading.

\section{What is new and what is not}

The idea beneath the whole stack comes from Sam Harris's \emph{The Moral
Landscape}~\cite{harris2010moral}. Harris argues that moral questions have better and worse answers
and that the answers turn on facts about the well-being of conscious creatures. He pictures the
possible ways of living as a landscape whose peaks are the heights of flourishing and whose valleys
are the depths of suffering, and he allows that it has more than one peak. Geometric Ethics began
from that picture of a moral space. It departs from it in one respect that the rest of this report
depends on. Harris measures height by well-being alone, and Chapter~\ref{ch:ge} argues that a single
number cannot carry a moral assessment, so the space here has several dimensions and is divided
into strata.
% src: owner 2026-10-14 ("we should include sam harris' book, the moral landscape. it inspired this entire concept"); Harris 2010 (Free Press, ISBN 978-1-4391-7121-9)

Most of the ideas the robot rests on have precedents, and Chapter~\ref{ch:related} credits them in
detail. An ethical governor that sits between proposal and actuation is Arkin's
architecture~\cite{arkin2009governing}, applied to care by Shim and Arkin~\cite{shim2017intervening}
and formally verified on a robot by Bremner and colleagues~\cite{bremner2019proactive}.
Declarative, defeasible norms for care robots, checked formally and enforced at run time, are the
subject of the SLEEC line of work~\cite{getiryaman2023sleec,desanctis2026sleecruntime}. The
principle that a language model cannot grant itself authority because provenance is tracked
outside it is that of the dual-model pattern~\cite{willison2023dualllm} and of
CaMeL~\cite{debenedetti2026camel}, and letting the model only select among permitted actions is the
action-selector pattern~\cite{beurerkellner2025patterns}. Formal guardrails for robots driven by
language models exist~\cite{yang2024safetychip,ravichandran2026roboguard}, and so does a deontic
run time for language-model agents~\cite{joshi2026deontic}.

What we did not find in prior work is the combination this report describes and measures. Authority
is gated on a quorum of attested physical sensor witnesses on independent substrates, with rights
that revert per right when the evidence lapses. The moral state is stratified, and its
authority-granting gates are provably closed to model-written events for any set of gates. The
whole is evaluated in an embodied simulation with several language-model agents and a sealed
held-out set, and the report says what that evaluation found, including our own errors. Our search
of prior work was broad but not systematic, and the claim stands only as far as it went.
